FICO basics, with a Danish example

What SAP FI and CO are, how a company is set up in SAP, and how one journal entry works. Told with one Danish furniture maker, Nordlys Møbler, and one rent payment.

Before you start

This is the first lesson of the FICO course, so you need no SAP or accounting background. If you took the FSCD course, its first lesson uses the same idea of a posting document, so some of this will feel familiar. All names, amounts, account numbers and codes below are made up for learning.

Meet the example: Nordlys Møbler A/S

Nordlys Møbler A/S is a furniture maker in Aarhus. It makes oak dining tables and chairs, buys oak timber from a supplier in Silkeborg and sells to furniture shops across Denmark. It runs SAP S/4HANA. In SAP the company is called company code DK01 and it keeps its books in Danish kroner (DKK).

You are Sofie Holm, a new business analyst (BA) in the finance team. In this course you follow Nordlys Møbler through 2026, one business event at a time.

1. What SAP FICO is, in simple words

SAP is one big system that runs a whole company: buying, selling, making things, paying people. It is split into modules, one per business area. FICO is two modules that work as a pair:

FI: Financial Accounting

In simple words: the company's official books. Every krone in and out is recorded so the company can produce its balance sheet, its profit and loss statement (P&L) and its VAT return. It looks outward: the tax agency, auditors, banks and owners read it.

Example: at year end, Peter Lund sends the annual report of Nordlys Møbler to the Danish Business Authority. The numbers come from FI.

CO: Controlling

In simple words: the company's internal view of the same money. It answers "which department spent what?" and "which product makes money?". It looks inward: managers read it to make decisions.

Example: Jonas Krogh wants to know if tables or chairs earn more. FI only says "the company made a profit". CO splits it by product.

1.1 Where FICO sits among the other modules

Most postings in FI and CO are not typed by finance people. They are created automatically when someone else does their job: a warehouse worker receives oak, a sales clerk bills a customer, the payroll run pays salaries. Every one of these events has a money effect, and that effect lands in FI and CO.

Green: events from other modules that create postings. Orange: reports built from those postings. Payroll is often run outside SAP and sends its totals in by interface.

This is why a FICO BA must understand a little of every other module. When Jonas asks "why is production so expensive this month?", the answer may start with a goods receipt in MM, not with anything finance did.

2. How a company is set up in SAP

Before anyone can post a single krone, SAP needs to know the organisational structure: which legal company it is, which accounts it uses, how its year is divided and how managers want to see costs. These settings are made once, at the start of a project, and are hard to change later. That is why BAs are asked about them early.

Why special periods?

After December is finished, the auditor may still find corrections, for example a forgotten bonus accrual. Instead of reopening December, they are posted in period 13 to 16 of the same year. December stays clean, and the annual figures still include the corrections.

3. Debit, credit and the GL account

3.1 Debit and credit in one minute

Accounting uses double entry: every business event is written down at least twice, once on the debit side of one account and once on the credit side of another, for the same total. Debit and credit do not mean "good" and "bad". They are simply the left and right side of an account.

Kind of accountGoes up with aGoes down with aNordlys Møbler example
Assets (what the company owns)DebitCreditMoney in Danske Bank, oak in the warehouse, the CNC machine
Liabilities (what it owes)CreditDebitUnpaid invoice from Jysk Træ, VAT owed to the tax agency
Equity (owners' share)CreditDebitShare capital, profit kept from earlier years
Expenses (costs)DebitCreditRent, electricity, depreciation
Revenue (income)CreditDebitSales of tables to Hygge Hjem

A "T-account" is how accountants sketch an account on paper: debits on the left, credits on the right.

How SAP shows it

In SAP reports a debit is a positive number and a credit is a negative number. So in the bank account's line items you will see -50,000.00 for the rent. Every document must add up to 0.00, otherwise SAP refuses to post it.

3.2 The GL account (FS00)

A GL account (general ledger account) is one "box" in the books where amounts of one kind are collected: bank, rent, sales. You create and view it in transaction FS00. Every GL account is one of two big kinds:

Balance sheet account

In simple words: things the company owns or owes on a given day. The balance carries forward into the next year.

Example: the bank balance on 31.12.2026 is the opening balance on 01.01.2027.

P&L account

In simple words: income and costs for a period. At year end the total profit or loss moves to a retained earnings account in equity, and the P&L accounts start again from zero.

Example: rent for 2026 adds up to DKK 600,000 (12 × 50,000). On 01.01.2027 account 470000 starts at zero.

Reconciliation account

In simple words: a summary account in the GL for a whole group of customers or suppliers. You never post to it directly. When Maria posts an invoice to the supplier Jysk Træ, SAP updates the supplier's own account and account 160000 at the same moment. So the GL always agrees with the supplier list.

Example: if Nordlys Møbler owes 40 suppliers a total of DKK 900,000, account 160000 shows 900,000 and the 40 supplier accounts add up to the same 900,000.

In S/4HANA, FS00 also holds the cost element setting that CO needs. A P&L account such as Rent is a primary cost element (the cost comes from outside the company). An account that only moves cost inside the company, such as 940000, is a secondary cost element and only lives in CO.

4. The journal entry, explained with a screen

A journal entry (in SAP: an accounting document) is the permanent record of one business event. It has a header (information about the whole document) and two or more line items (one per account touched). Once posted it is never deleted. A mistake is fixed by posting a reversal document.

The story: on 25.02.2026 the landlord sends the rent invoice for March. On 01.03.2026 Maria Berg pays DKK 50,000 from Danske Bank and posts it with transaction FB50 (enter a GL account document). Sofie opens the result in FB03 (Display Document).

What each number means:

  • 1

    Document number: the unique ID of this entry inside company code DK01 and fiscal year 2026. Numbers come from a number range per document type. Always quote it: "please check document 100000017 in DK01, 2026".

  • 2

    Company code: whose books it is in. DK01, Nordlys Møbler.

  • 3

    Fiscal year: together with document number and company code, it makes the document unique. The same number can exist again in 2027.

  • 4

    Document type: what kind of entry this is. SA is a plain GL account document. Others you will meet: KR (supplier invoice), DR (customer invoice), AA (asset posting). The type controls the number range and which accounts are allowed.

  • 5

    Document date: the date on the original paper, here the landlord's invoice (25.02).

  • 6

    Posting date: the date the entry counts in the books. It decides the period. This is the date finance cares about.

  • 7

    Period: derived from the posting date and the fiscal year variant. 01.03 is period 3 (March). Period 3 must be open in OB52, or SAP refuses the posting.

  • 8

    Currency: Danish kroner. A euro invoice would show EUR here and SAP would also store the DKK value.

  • 9

    Reference: the outside document's number, here the landlord's invoice number. Very useful when searching.

  • 10

    Entered by: the user who posted it (Maria). For automatic postings you see a batch or interface user.

  • 11

    Posting key (PK): a two-digit code that says debit or credit and what kind of account. 40 = debit to a GL account, 50 = credit to a GL account. (For suppliers and customers other keys are used, for example 31 for a supplier invoice.)

  • 12

    Account: which GL account the line goes to: 470000 Rent and 110000 Bank.

  • 13

    Amount: positive for debit, negative for credit. The lines add up to 0.00, so the document balances.

  • 14

    Cost centre: the CO part of the line. Rent is a cost, so SAP asks "which department?". Answer: 3000 Administration. The bank line has none, because a bank account is not a cost.

  • 15

    Text: a short note for humans. Good texts save hours of investigation later.

Document date vs posting date: the classic question

If the landlord's invoice (document date 25.02) were posted with posting date 28.02, the cost would count in February. Posted on 01.03, it counts in March. Same paper, different month. That choice is why month-end reports can surprise people.

5. The Universal Journal (ACDOCA)

In older SAP systems (SAP ECC), FI and CO kept their own tables, and finance spent days checking that they agreed. In S/4HANA there is one table for both: the Universal Journal, table ACDOCA. Every line item of every posting is one row in it, and that row carries both the FI view (company code, GL account, amount) and the CO view (cost centre, profit centre, order).

One table in the middle: legal reports and management reports read the same rows, so they cannot disagree.

Our rent document 100000017 becomes two rows in ACDOCA, roughly like this (simplified; the real table has hundreds of columns):

Simplified. A cost centre is linked to a profit centre in its master data, so SAP can fill the profit centre automatically. Administration costs are often kept on a separate "central" profit centre.

What this means for you as a BA: there is one version of the truth. When Jonas's cost centre report and Peter's P&L show different rent totals, the difference is in the selection (dates, accounts, cost centres), not in two separate ledgers.

Debits: 50,000 + 18,000 + 80,000 + 20,000 + 150,000 + 5,000 = 323,000.
Credits: 50,000 + 18,000 + 100,000 + 120,000 + 30,000 + 5,000 = 323,000. Equal, as they must be.

March result so far: revenue 120,000 minus rent 50,000, electricity 18,000 and depreciation 5,000 = DKK 47,000 profit. The oak is not a cost yet: it sits in the warehouse as an asset until it is used to make tables.

Bank: only postings 1 and 2 touch the bank, so it goes down by 50,000 + 18,000 = DKK 68,000. The supplier and the customer have not paid or been paid yet: that money sits on 160000 and 140000 as open items.

7. What a FICO BA does, plus a BA toolkit

A FICO BA stands between finance people (Peter, Maria, Jonas) and the SAP team. Typical work: write down how a business process should run in SAP, decide which accounts and cost objects each posting needs, write and run test cases, explain differences in reports, and help users during month-end. You do not need to be an accountant, but you must be able to read a journal entry and ask good questions.

7.1 Questions to ask

  • Which company codes are in scope, and do they share a chart of accounts and a controlling area?
  • Is the fiscal year the calendar year? Who opens and closes posting periods, and when?
  • For each cost (rent, electricity, IT), which cost centre should carry it, and who is responsible for that cost centre?
  • Which profit figures does management want: per product line, region or customer group?
  • Which postings come automatically from other modules or interfaces, and which are typed by hand?
  • Which document types are used, and who may post each one?
  • What VAT codes are needed (25% Danish VAT, EU purchases, exports)?

A closing tip

Pick one real document in your test system and explain every field out loud, like the FB03 screen above: who, when, which period, which accounts, which cost centre, why it balances. If you can do that, you can follow any FICO conversation. Every later lesson in this course is just more journal entries with a story behind them.
