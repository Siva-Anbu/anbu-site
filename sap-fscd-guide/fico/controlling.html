Controlling: costs, cost centres and profitability

Financial accounting tells you what the company spent. Controlling tells you who spent it, why, and which products really make money. Follow Jonas, the controller, through March at Nordlys Møbler.

1. FI and CO: same postings, different questions

FI (Financial Accounting) is the official, legal view: balance sheet, profit and loss, VAT, what the auditors and tax authorities see. It answers "what did we spend, and on what kind of thing?" CO (Controlling) is the internal management view. It answers "who spent it, for which purpose, and what did it earn?" Nobody outside the company sees CO, so the company can design it the way managers think.

In older SAP systems FI and CO kept separate tables and needed a nightly reconciliation. In S/4HANA they share one table, the Universal Journal (ACDOCA). Every cost line carries both the GL account (for FI) and a cost object such as a cost centre, an internal order or a profit centre (for CO). One posting, two ways of looking at it.

There is no separate "CO copy" of the rent. FI and CO read the same line, which is why they agree by design.

2. Cost elements: primary and secondary

A cost element says what kind of cost it is: rent, electricity, salaries. In S/4HANA a cost element is simply a GL account (maintained in FS00) that has a cost element category. The category tells CO how the account may be used. There are two families.

Primary cost element

In simple words: a cost (or revenue) that comes from outside the company and is posted in FI. Every primary cost element is a P&L account.

Example: the rent invoice of DKK 50,000 on account 470000. The money really leaves the company.

Secondary cost element

In simple words: a cost that only moves inside CO, from one department to another. No money leaves the company and FI's profit does not change.

Example: 940000 Secondary cost: IT assessment, used when the IT department's costs are shared out to the other departments (section 5).

Balance sheet accounts such as 110000 Bank or 160000 Trade payables are not cost elements: they never carry a cost centre.

BA tip: "cost element required"

If an account is a primary cost element, SAP insists on a cost object when you post to it. Posting rent without a cost centre gives an error like "account 470000 requires an assignment to a CO object". Users meet this message often; it is a feature, not a bug.

3. Cost centres and the standard hierarchy

A cost centre is a place in the company where costs arise and someone is responsible for them: usually a department. Nordlys Møbler has four. Each cost centre belongs to the controlling area NM00, has a manager, a validity period and a category (production, sales, administration, service).

All cost centres must hang in one tree called the standard hierarchy. Its groups let Jonas report on "all of Administration and IT" with one click. You create cost centres with KS01; the hierarchy itself is maintained in its own screen (OKEON in classic GUI).

Group names are made up. Real companies often mirror the organisation chart, so a re-organisation means a hierarchy change.

To see what landed on a cost centre, Jonas uses KSB1 (cost centre actual line items): every posting with date, account, amount and document number. It is the first place to look when a manager asks "why is this cost on my department?"

Plan versus actual

Planning simply means writing down, before the year starts, what each cost centre is expected to cost. The rent contract says DKK 600,000 a year, so Jonas plans DKK 50,000 a month on cost centre 3000. During the year SAP compares the plan with the actual postings, and the variance (actual minus plan) shows where to look. Many S/4HANA projects plan in SAP Analytics Cloud or Fiori apps; older systems used KP06.

4. Internal orders: collecting costs for a temporary job

Cost centres are permanent. But some costs belong to a temporary event or project, and managers want to see its total cost on its own. For that SAP has the internal order: a short-lived cost collector with its own number, a budget and an end date. You create one with KO01.

Nordlys Møbler will show its new table range at Formland, the design fair in Herning. Jonas creates order 700100 "Formland trade fair 2026" with a budget of DKK 80,000. Every invoice for the fair is posted with order 700100 instead of a cost centre.

Check it yourself

40,000 + 10,000 + 15,000 = 65,000. Budget 80,000 − actual 65,000 = 15,000 left. If a fourth invoice of DKK 20,000 had arrived, the total would be 85,000 and SAP's availability control could warn or block the posting, because it is over the budget.

Settlement: emptying the order

An internal order is only a temporary bucket. At month-end Jonas settles it: SAP moves the balance to the receiver named in the order's settlement rule, here cost centre 2000 Sales, because the fair is a sales activity. He runs KO88 (one order; KO8G settles many at once). Settlement is a CO posting: the order is credited and the cost centre is debited. Depending on the settlement profile it keeps the original cost elements or uses a settlement cost element (a secondary one). Once the fair is over and all invoices are in, the order is closed so nobody can post to it again.

5. Allocations: sharing IT costs by headcount

Cost centre 4100 IT has DKK 24,000 of costs in March: licences, laptops and support. IT does not sell anything; it serves the other departments. To show the true cost of each department, Jonas shares the IT cost out. This is an allocation, and the most common kind is an assessment.

He needs a fair key. IT work roughly follows the number of people, so he uses headcount, stored in SAP as a statistical key figure (a number, not money, posted per cost centre).

The sender is credited and the receivers are debited, all on secondary cost element 940000. Total cost in the company does not change.

Check it yourself

15,000 + 5,000 + 4,000 = 24,000, exactly what IT spent. After the assessment cost centre 4100 shows 24,000 − 24,000 = 0. The P&L in FI is unchanged: the money was spent once, CO only decided who carries it.

Cycles: KSU1 and KSU5

The rule "share 4100 by headcount to 1000, 2000 and 3000" is saved once as an assessment cycle with KSU1. A cycle holds one or more segments: sender, receivers, the key (headcount) and the secondary cost element 940000. Every month-end Jonas runs it with KSU5, first as a test run to check the amounts, then for real. If headcount changes in April, he updates the statistical key figure; the cycle stays the same.

6. Profit centres and margin analysis

Cost centres answer "what does each department cost?". Jonas's second question, "do tables or chairs make money?", needs revenue as well as cost. Two tools answer it.

Profit centre

In simple words: a part of the business that is run like a small company of its own, with revenue, costs and profit. Every cost centre, product and order is linked to one, so postings find their profit centre automatically.

Example: Nordlys Møbler has PC-TABLES (dining tables) and PC-CHAIRS (chairs). The table billing to Hygge Hjem lands on PC-TABLES because the product master points there.

Margin analysis (account-based CO-PA)

In simple words: profitability analysis that slices revenue and cost by characteristics such as customer, product, region and sales channel. In S/4HANA it is account-based, so it reads the same GL accounts in the Universal Journal.

Example: "What margin did we make on oak tables sold to Hygge Hjem in the Copenhagen region in March?"

March margin by profit centre

In March Nordlys Møbler sold the 10 tables to Hygge Hjem (Lesson 3) and 40 chairs at DKK 1,500 to several smaller shops. When goods leave the warehouse, SAP posts the cost of goods sold (COGS): what the products cost to make, mostly oak and labour. Revenue minus COGS is the gross margin.

Both lines make money, and tables do slightly better per krone. Notice that this is a gross margin: the rent, IT and the Formland fair still sit on cost centres. Many companies later assign those overheads to profitability segments too, to see a net margin per product. That is a design decision for Jonas, not a technical must.

Jonas reads margin analysis with KE30 (run a profitability report) and drills down from "all customers" to Hygge Hjem to single documents. For profit centres he uses KE5Z (profit centre line items), or the Fiori apps that read the same ACDOCA lines.

7. Month-end in controlling

After FI has posted everything for March (invoices, depreciation, accruals), CO does its own short list. The order matters: senders must be complete before they are shared out.

  1. 01.04: check that all March invoices carry a cost object; fix wrong cost centres with a repost (KB11N) rather than a new FI posting.
  2. 02.04: settle internal orders (KO88 or KO8G). Order 700100 sends DKK 65,000 to cost centre 2000.
  3. 02.04: run assessment cycles (KSU5), test run first. IT's DKK 24,000 is shared to 1000, 2000 and 3000.
  4. 03.04: plan/actual reports per cost centre (S_ALR_87013611) and margin reports (KE30). Managers explain big variances.
  5. 03.04: close the CO period (OKP1) so nobody posts to March by mistake.

Check it yourself: does CO still agree with FI?

The story's costs in FI are 97,000 (on cost centres) + 65,000 (the fair, first on order 700100) = 162,000. The cost centre totals after month-end are 38,000 + 70,000 + 54,000 + 0 = 162,000. The assessment column sums to 0 and settlement only moved the fair's cost. Because FI and CO both read ACDOCA, they agree by design; there is no separate FI/CO reconciliation step like in older systems.

8. BA toolkit for controlling

8.1 Questions to ask

  • Which departments need their own cost centre, and who is the responsible manager for each?
  • What does the standard hierarchy look like, and who approves changes when the organisation changes?
  • Which GL accounts are primary cost elements, and which secondary cost elements do we need for allocations?
  • Which temporary events or projects need an internal order? What budget, and should SAP warn or block when it is exceeded?
  • Who receives each order at settlement, and when is an order closed?
  • Which costs are shared out, by which key (headcount, square metres, hours), and in which order do the cycles run?
  • Which characteristics does margin analysis need: customer, product, region, sales channel? Should overheads reach profitability?
  • Where is planning done, and at what level of detail?

A closing tip

When a manager says "these numbers are wrong", start with one line item in KSB1 and follow it: which account, which cost centre, where did the cost centre come from, and was it moved later by settlement or assessment? Nine times out of ten the answer is a missing or wrong cost object, not a broken report.
