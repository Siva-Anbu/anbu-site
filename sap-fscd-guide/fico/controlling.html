Controlling: costs, lines of business and the combined ratio

Financial accounting tells you what the insurer spent. Controlling tells you who spent it, why, and whether motor or home insurance earns an underwriting profit. Follow Jonas, the controller, through March at Nordica Forsikring.

Before you start

This is Lesson 4 of the FICO course. It builds on Lesson 1: FICO basics (the general ledger, GL accounts, company code NF01 and the Universal Journal). It reuses numbers from Lesson 2: accounts payable (the laptops), Lesson 3: premiums and receivables (earned premium) and Lesson 5: assets, reserves and close (the claims centre fit-out), but you can follow this lesson on its own. The profit and loss statement looks fine. But Jonas Krogh, the controller, is not happy with one total. 1. FI and CO: same postings, different questions

FI (Financial Accounting) is the official, legal view: balance sheet, profit and loss, what the auditors, Finanstilsynet (the Danish FSA) and Skattestyrelsen (the tax agency) see. It answers "what did we spend, and on what kind of thing?" CO (Controlling) is the internal management view. It answers "who spent it, for which purpose, and what did it earn?" Nobody outside the company sees CO, so the company can design it the way managers think.

In older SAP systems FI and CO kept separate tables and needed a reconciliation. In S/4HANA they share one table, the Universal Journal (ACDOCA). Every cost line carries both the GL account (for FI) and a cost object such as a cost centre, an internal order or a profit centre (for CO). One posting, two ways of looking at it.

There is no separate "CO copy" of the rent. FI and CO read the same line, which is why they agree by design.

2. Cost elements: primary and secondary

A cost element says what kind of cost it is: rent, telephony, depreciation. In S/4HANA a cost element is simply a GL account (maintained in FS00) that has a cost element category. The category tells CO how the account may be used. There are two families.

Primary cost element

In simple words: a cost (or revenue) that comes from outside the company and is posted in FI. Every primary cost element is a P&L account.

Example: the rent invoice of DKK 50,000 on account 470000. The money really leaves the company.

Secondary cost element

In simple words: a cost that only moves inside CO, from one department to another. No money leaves the company and FI's profit does not change.

Example: 940000 Secondary cost: IT assessment, used when the IT department's costs are shared out to the other departments (section 5).

Balance sheet accounts such as 110000 Bank, 160000 Trade payables or 260000 Claims reserves are not cost elements: they never carry a cost centre. Note that Nordica cannot deduct VAT, so the VAT on a purchase is part of the cost on the same account.

BA tip: "cost object required"

If an account is a primary cost element, SAP insists on a cost object when you post to it. Posting rent without a cost centre gives an error like "account 470000 requires an assignment to a CO object". Users meet this message often; it is a feature, not a bug.

3. Cost centres and the standard hierarchy

A cost centre is a place in the company where costs arise and someone is responsible for them: usually a department. Nordica has four. Each belongs to the controlling area NF00, has a manager, a validity period and a category (for example administration or sales).

All cost centres must hang in one tree called the standard hierarchy. Its groups let Jonas report on "all of Administration and IT" with one click. You create cost centres with KS01; the hierarchy itself is maintained in its own screen (OKEON in classic GUI).

Group names are made up. Real companies often mirror the organisation chart, so a re-organisation means a hierarchy change.

Why an insurer splits costs into three types

A factory has one big "cost of goods sold". An insurer has none. Its biggest cost is claims, and the rest of its costs are split by purpose. The usual split is:

Expense typeIn simple wordsNordica example
Claims handling costs (loss adjustment expenses)The cost of dealing with claims: handlers, call centre, claims ITCost centre 1000: telephony, the 40 laptops, the claims centre depreciation
Acquisition costsThe cost of winning and renewing policies: advertising, sales staff, broker commissionCost centre 2000 and order 700100 (the spring motor campaign)
Administration costsEverything else needed to run the companyCost centre 3000 (rent) and 4100 (IT, once shared out)

Why bother? Because the combined ratio (section 6), the statutory reports to Finanstilsynet and the IFRS 17 reporting all need these types separately. Claims handling costs are often counted with claims, acquisition costs are often tied to new policies, and administration is reported on its own. A good cost centre design makes the split almost automatic: the cost centre already tells you the type. Treat the table above as an example and confirm the exact split with your finance team, because your actuarial and finance colleagues own the reporting rules.

To see what landed on a cost centre, Jonas uses KSB1 (cost centre actual line items): every posting with date, account, amount and document number. It is the first place to look when a manager asks "why is this cost on my department?"

Plan versus actual

Planning simply means writing down, before the year starts, what each cost centre is expected to cost. The rent contract says DKK 600,000 a year, so Jonas plans DKK 50,000 a month on cost centre 3000. During the year SAP compares the plan with the actual postings, and the variance (actual minus plan) shows where to look. 4. Internal orders: collecting costs for a temporary job

Cost centres are permanent. But some costs belong to a temporary event or project, and managers want to see its total cost on its own. For that SAP has the internal order: a short-lived cost collector with its own number, a budget and an end date. You create one with KO01.

In spring Nordica runs a marketing push for car insurance. Jonas creates order 700100 "Spring motor campaign 2026" with a budget of DKK 80,000. Every invoice for the campaign is posted with order 700100 instead of a cost centre. This is an acquisition cost: it is meant to win new motor policies.

Check it yourself

40,000 + 10,000 + 15,000 = 65,000. Budget 80,000 − actual 65,000 = 15,000 left. If a fourth invoice of DKK 20,000 arrived, the total would be 85,000 and SAP's availability control could warn or block the posting, because it is over budget.

Settlement: emptying the order

An internal order is only a temporary bucket. At month-end Jonas settles it: SAP moves the balance to the receiver named in the order's settlement rule, here cost centre 2000 Sales and distribution, because the campaign is a sales activity. He runs KO88 (one order; KO8G settles many at once). Settlement is a CO posting: the order is credited and the cost centre is debited. Depending on the settlement profile it keeps the original cost elements or uses a settlement cost element (a secondary one). Once the campaign is over and all invoices are in, the order is closed so nobody can post to it again.

5. Allocations: sharing IT costs by headcount

Cost centre 4100 IT has DKK 24,000 of costs in March: licences and support. IT does not sell insurance; it serves the other departments. To show the true cost of each department, Jonas shares the IT cost out. This is an allocation, and the most common kind is an assessment.

He needs a fair key. IT work roughly follows the number of people, so he uses headcount, stored in SAP as a statistical key figure (a number, not money, posted per cost centre).

The sender is credited and the receivers are debited, all on secondary cost element 940000. Total cost in the company does not change.

Cycles: KSU1 and KSU5

The rule "share 4100 by headcount to 1000, 2000 and 3000" is saved once as an assessment cycle with KSU1. A cycle holds one or more segments: sender, receivers, the key (headcount) and the secondary cost element 940000. Every month-end Jonas runs it with KSU5, first as a test run to check the amounts, then for real. If headcount changes in April, he updates the statistical key figure; the cycle stays the same.

6. Profit centres, lines of business and the combined ratio

Cost centres answer "what does each department cost?". Jonas's second question, "does motor or home make money?", needs premium and claims as well as cost. Two tools answer it.

Profit centre

In simple words: a part of the business run like a small company of its own, with income, costs and a result. For an insurer the natural profit centre is a line of business.

Example: Nordica has PC-MOTOR (car insurance) and PC-HOME (home and property). The Hygge Hjem commercial property policy ERH-2026-0042 sits in PC-HOME because the product points there.

Margin analysis (account-based CO-PA)

In simple words: profitability analysis that slices premium and cost by characteristics such as line of business, product, sales channel (direct, broker, partner) and region. In S/4HANA it is account-based, so it reads the same GL accounts in the Universal Journal.

Example: "What was the result of motor insurance sold through brokers in Region Hovedstaden in March?"

Earned premium, claims incurred and the ratios

Insurers do not compare "sales" with "cost of goods". They use four ideas. Line Madsen, the actuary, owns most of them.

  • Earned premium: the part of the premium that belongs to the months already passed. Gross written premium minus the change in the unearned premium reserve (UPR). Example: Hygge Hjem's DKK 120,000 annual premium earns DKK 10,000 a month.
  • Claims incurred: claims paid plus the change in claims reserves (RBNS and IBNR, estimated by Line). It is what the March business will cost, not only what was paid.
  • Operating expenses: the three expense types from section 3, after the allocations of this lesson.
  • Technical result: earned premium − claims incurred − operating expenses.

Amounts in thousand DKK. Total combined ratio: 66.25% + 21.875% = 88.125%, shown as 88.1%.

Below 100% is an underwriting profit

A combined ratio of 100% means that for every krone of premium earned, one krone went on claims and costs. Below 100%, the insurance business itself earns money: Motor keeps 10 øre of every krone and Home keeps 15 øre. Above 100% the insurer makes an underwriting loss and has to rely on investment income to stay profitable. Note that Home has the lower loss ratio but the higher expense ratio, so cost allocation matters for the answer.

Check it yourself

Technical result: 1,600,000 − 1,060,000 − 350,000 = 190,000 (Motor 100,000 + Home 90,000). Add March investment income of DKK 40,000 (bond interest and value changes) and the result before tax is 230,000. Also: 100% − 88.125% = 11.875%, and 11.875% of 1,600,000 is 190,000.

7. Month-end in controlling

After FI has posted everything for March (invoices, depreciation, accruals, the FS-CD daily totals and the actuary's reserve entries), CO does its own short list. The order matters: senders must be complete before they are shared out.

  1. 01.04: check that all March invoices carry a cost object; fix wrong cost centres with a repost (KB11N) rather than a new FI posting.
  2. 02.04: settle internal orders (KO88 or KO8G). Order 700100 sends DKK 65,000 to cost centre 2000.
  3. 02.04: run the IT assessment cycle (KSU5), test run first. IT's DKK 24,000 is shared to 1000, 2000 and 3000.
  4. 03.04: allocate cost centres to the lines of business (second cycle), then run plan/actual reports (S_ALR_87013611) and margin reports (KE30). Line checks the ratios; managers explain big variances.
  5. 03.04: close the CO period (OKP1) so nobody posts to March by mistake.
Check it yourself: does CO still agree with FI?

Operating expenses in the FI profit and loss are 350,000: 285,000 posted directly on cost centres plus 65,000 on order 700100. The cost centre totals after month-end are 178,000 + 90,000 + 82,000 + 0 = 350,000, and the two lines of business add up to 200,000 + 150,000 = 350,000. The assessment column sums to 0 and settlement only moved the campaign's cost. Because FI and CO both read ACDOCA, they agree by design; there is no separate FI/CO reconciliation step like in older systems.

8. BA toolkit for controlling

8.1 Questions to ask

  • Which departments need their own cost centre, and who is the responsible manager for each?
  • How do the cost centres map to claims handling, acquisition and administration costs? Who in finance confirms the split?
  • What does the standard hierarchy look like, and who approves changes when the organisation changes?
  • Which GL accounts are primary cost elements, and which secondary cost elements do we need for allocations?
  • Which campaigns or projects need an internal order? What budget, and should SAP warn or block when it is exceeded?
  • Who receives each order at settlement, and when is an order closed?
  • Which keys share costs (headcount, claim counts, premium), and in which order do the cycles run?
  • Which lines of business are profit centres? Which characteristics does margin analysis need: product, channel, region?
  • Who owns the combined ratio definition: finance or the actuarial team?

A closing tip

When a manager says "these numbers are wrong", start with one line item in KSB1 and follow it: which account, which cost centre, where did the cost centre come from, and was it moved later by settlement or assessment? Nine times out of ten the answer is a missing or wrong cost object, not a broken report. For ratios, ask Line first which definition of "operating expenses" is being used.
