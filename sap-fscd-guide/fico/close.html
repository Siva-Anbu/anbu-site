1. What a fixed asset is

A fixed asset is something a company buys to use, not to sell, and that it will use for more than one year. For an insurer these are typically office fit-outs, buildings, servers and company cars. Laptops are usually too cheap: Lesson 2 expensed them at once, because they are under the small-asset limit (confirm the limit with your team).

Why not call the fit-out a DKK 600,000 expense in January? Then January would look like a terrible month and the next nine years would look too good. The offices help the claims handlers for ten years, so the cost is spread over ten years. That spreading is called depreciation.

Depreciation

In simple words: each period, a slice of the asset's cost moves from the balance sheet (what the company owns) to the P&L (what it cost to run the business).

Example: DKK 600,000 ÷ 10 years = DKK 60,000 a year, or DKK 5,000 a month. This "same amount every period" method is called straight-line depreciation.

Book value (net book value)

In simple words: what the asset is still worth in the books: cost minus all depreciation so far.

Example: after one year, 600,000 − 60,000 = DKK 540,000. After ten years the book value is zero, even if the offices still look fine.

Straight-line depreciation of the fit-out: the same DKK 60,000 slice every year until the book value reaches zero at the end of 2035.

Why DKK 600,000 and not 480,000?

Insurance is exempt from VAT in Denmark (momsfritaget). Nordica charges no VAT on premiums, and in general it cannot deduct the VAT it pays on purchases (financial companies pay lønsumsafgift, a payroll tax, instead). So the 25% VAT is simply part of the cost. Kontorbyg A/S invoiced DKK 480,000 plus DKK 120,000 VAT, and the whole DKK 600,000 becomes the asset's cost. Confirm the exact tax treatment with your tax team.

2.1 Depreciation areas: one asset, two sets of rules

The financial statements and the Danish tax return don't depreciate an asset in the same way. Tax rules differ, so SAP keeps a separate depreciation area for each purpose. Each area has its own useful life, method and values for the same asset.

2.2 The asset sub-ledger

Remember suppliers (Lesson 2) and policyholders (Lesson 3)? Each has its own detail (a sub-ledger), and the general ledger only sees the total on a reconciliation account. Assets work the same way. The asset sub-ledger knows every asset one by one. The general ledger only sees the totals on 211000 Office fit-out and equipment and 211900 Accumulated depreciation. Nobody posts to 211000 directly; every posting goes through an asset, so the two always agree.

The asset class points to 211000, 211900 and 481000 (this setup is called account determination). In S/4HANA all of it lands in the Universal Journal (ACDOCA).

3. Buying the asset (01.01.2026)

Nordica's claims centre in Valby was fitted out by Kontorbyg A/S (BP 300180). The invoice was DKK 480,000 plus 25% VAT of DKK 120,000. Because the invoice line points to asset 10000045 instead of a normal GL account, SAP updates the asset and the general ledger in one go. This is called an integrated acquisition: the asset and the supplier are posted in one document.

4. The monthly depreciation run (AFAB)

Depreciation is not typed in by hand. Every month a batch program, the depreciation run (AFAB), calculates the planned amount for every asset and posts it to the general ledger. Peter runs it first as a test run, checks the list, then runs it for real. For the fit-out it posts:

AccountDebit / CreditDKK
481000 Depreciation (cost centre 1000)Debit5,000.00
211900 Accumulated depreciationCredit5,000.00

Notice that 211000 is not touched: the original cost stays visible, and the depreciation piles up on its own account. Book value = 211000 minus 211900.

4.2 Selling or scrapping an asset

When an asset leaves the company, it is retired. SAP removes its cost and accumulated depreciation, and compares the book value with what the company got for it. Imagine Nordica moves out of Valby on 31.12.2028, after three years, and the fit-out is scrapped:

StepDKK
Cost600,000.00
Accumulated depreciation (36 months × 5,000)−180,000.00
Book value at retirement420,000.00
Proceeds0.00
Loss on retirement (0 − 420,000)−420,000.00

If the landlord paid DKK 100,000 for the fit-out, the loss would be DKK 320,000. For an asset that is sold, such as a company car, it works the same way: proceeds above the book value are a gain, proceeds below it are a loss.

4.3 Assets under construction

Some assets take months to build, for example a new claims centre in Aarhus. While it's being built, costs are collected on an asset under construction (AuC). An AuC is not depreciated, because it isn't in use yet. When the centre opens, the AuC is settled to a normal asset, and depreciation starts from then.

5. Insurance reserves at month-end

This is the part that is special for an insurer. A normal company records what has happened. An insurer must also record promises: cover it has been paid for but not yet delivered, and claims that have happened but are not paid yet. These promises are called reserves (hensættelser) and they appear as liabilities on the balance sheet. Two kinds matter at month-end.

The numbers are the company totals for March 2026. Each reserve is a liability on the balance sheet and a cost or a reduction of income in the P&L.

5.1 Unearned premium reserve (UPR)

In Lesson 3, Hygge Hjem A/S paid DKK 120,000 on 13.03 for a commercial property policy (ERH-2026-0042) that covers a whole year, 01.03.2026 to 28.02.2027. Nordica has not earned all of that yet. At 31.03 only one month of twelve has been delivered:

Hygge Hjem policy at 31.03.2026DKK
Annual premium (the 1.1% insurance tax of 1,320 is not Nordica's money, see 6.5)120,000.00
Earned: 1 month of 12 (120,000 ÷ 12)10,000.00
Unearned: 11 months, goes into the UPR110,000.00

For the whole company the written premium in March was DKK 2,400,000, and the change in UPR was −800,000, so the earned premium is 1,600,000. The journal moves the unearned part out of income and into a liability account:

5.2 Claims reserves: RBNS and IBNR

Claims are paid after they happen, sometimes months or years after. At month-end Nordica must still count them as a cost of March, because the accident happened in March. That money is set aside as claims reserves (erstatningshensættelser).

RBNS: reported but not settled

In simple words: the policyholder has told us about the claim, but we haven't paid it yet. The claims handler estimates the cost case by case.

Example: a customer reports a hail-damaged car on 27.03. The handler expects DKK 30,000 and sets that as the reserve. Across all open cases, RBNS went up by DKK 50,000 in March.

IBNR: incurred but not reported

In simple words: the accident has happened, but the customer hasn't told us yet. Nobody can estimate it case by case, so the actuary estimates the total from history and statistics.

Example: a water leak that happened on 29.03 in a house whose owners are on holiday. Actuary Line Madsen's model raised IBNR by DKK 250,000 in March.

31.03.2026, company totalDebit / CreditDKK
405000 Change in claims reservesDebit300,000.00
260000 Claims reserves (RBNS and IBNR)Credit300,000.00

Change in reserves = RBNS +50,000 + IBNR +250,000 = 300,000. Claims incurred = claims paid 760,000 + 300,000 = 1,060,000.

5.3 Who owns the numbers, and who posts them

NumberOwner (decides the amount)Posting
UPRActuarial and finance, from the policy system's premium dataMonthly journal entry, often loaded from a file or interface
RBNSClaims handlers, case by case in the claims systemMonthly journal from the claims system's totals
IBNRActuary (Line Madsen), from statistical modelsMonthly journal entry, approved by the actuary and Peter

Finance does not invent these figures. Claims and actuarial own the amounts, and finance posts them, checks that they match the source reports, and keeps the audit trail. Most insurers receive them as a ready-made journal from the actuarial models and load it as a manual posting (FB50 or a Fiori journal entry app) or through an interface. Note that these reserves are posted as the change for the month. They are not reversed on 01.04 like an accrual.

Reinsurance, briefly: Nordica buys cover from reinsurers such as Rhein Re AG for very large losses. The reinsurer's share of the reserves and claims is recorded too (for example receivables from reinsurers on 141000), so the P&L can show the result both gross and net of reinsurance. Your actuarial and finance team owns the rules for this.

6. The month-end close checklist

Closing a month means: make sure everything that belongs in March is in March, check it, then lock March so nobody can change it. Peter's checklist has twelve steps. The order matters, because later steps use the results of earlier ones.

Peter's close for March 2026. Real insurers often have 50 or more tasks and track them in a closing tool (SAP has the Financial Closing cockpit, called Advanced Financial Closing in newer releases).

6.1 Steps 1 to 3: is everything in?

  1. 1. FS-CD runs finished and transferred (01.04): premium billing, incoming payments and claims payouts are processed in FS-CD, which posts daily totals to the general ledger. Sofie checks that the March transfers are complete and that the balance on 140000 (premium receivables) equals the open items in FS-CD. Hygge Hjem's premium was billed on 01.03 and paid on 13.03, so it is cleared. See the FS-CD accounting lesson for how the transfer works. Ordinary supplier invoices (FI accounts payable) are all posted too: Maria checks her in-tray.
  2. 2. Bank reconciled (01.04): the last March bank statement from Danske Bank is loaded, and the balance on 110000 matches the bank's closing balance to the krone. Nothing is left on 113100 Bank incoming clearing.
  3. 3. GR/IR reviewed (01.04): Sofie lists open items on 191100 GR/IR clearing. The 40 laptops for claims handlers (goods received 05.03, invoiced 08.03) have cleared. Anything left over is either goods received but not yet invoiced, or invoiced but not yet received. Each one gets an owner and an explanation.

6.2 Step 4: accruals

Telephony for the claims call centre (DKK 18,000) was already billed and paid on 04.03, so it needs nothing here. But Nordica's external actuarial consultant helped with the March reporting, and her invoice of DKK 12,000 won't arrive until mid-April. If Nordica waits for the invoice, March looks DKK 12,000 too cheap and April too expensive. So Peter posts an accrual: a cost for March, owed to someone, without an invoice yet. The cost goes to 476500 Accounting, actuarial and audit fees, on cost centre 3000 Administration. The other side goes to 190000 Accrued expenses (a liability: money owed but not yet invoiced).

31.03.2026Debit / CreditDKK
476500 Accounting, actuarial and audit fees (cost centre 3000)Debit12,000.00
190000 Accrued expensesCredit12,000.00

The entry is posted with a reversal date of 01.04.2026. On 01.04 SAP posts the exact opposite. When the real invoice arrives in April and is posted to 476500, the reversal and the invoice cancel out, so April carries no actuarial cost for March. March shows the cost once; April doesn't show it twice. Compare this with the reserves in section 5: reserves are not reversed, because they are estimates that are updated each month.

6.3 Steps 5 and 6: depreciation and foreign currency

Depreciation: Peter runs AFAB for period 03.2026 as described in section 4.

Foreign currency valuation (FAGL_FCV): on 20.03 Nordica was billed a reinsurance premium by Rhein Re AG (BP 500010) of EUR 10,000. On that day the rate was 7.46, so the payable to the reinsurer (165000) was booked at DKK 74,600. On 31.03 the rate is 7.45. The balance sheet must show what Nordica would owe at today's rate:

CalculationDKK
Booked: 10,000 × 7.4674,600.00
Valued at month-end: 10,000 × 7.4574,500.00
Difference: Nordica owes less, an unrealised gain100.00

FAGL_FCV posts the DKK 100: the payable goes down and an unrealised exchange rate gain is credited to the P&L. "Unrealised" means it isn't real money yet: the rate can move again before Nordica pays. Many companies reverse the valuation on the first day of the next month and value again at the next month-end. The accounts used are set up in configuration.

6.4 Steps 7 and 8: reserves and CO allocations

Reserves: now that the premium and claims data for March are final, the UPR, RBNS and IBNR journals from section 5 are posted. They need the FS-CD transfer in step 1 to be complete, because the written premium and claims paid come from there.

CO allocations: now that all March costs are in (including the accrual and depreciation), Jonas runs the IT assessment and the other cycles from Lesson 4, and settles internal order 700100 "Spring motor campaign 2026" to cost centre 2000. Running them earlier would have missed costs.

6.5 Steps 9 and 10: investments and insurance tax

Investments valued: Nordica invests the premiums it holds in bonds until claims must be paid. At month-end the investment team's bonds are valued at market price and interest earned is booked. In March this gave DKK 40,000 of investment income (bond interest and value changes) on 600000. The investment team owns the valuation; finance posts and checks it.

Insurance tax: premiums for non-life insurance carry an insurance tax (skadesforsikringsafgift). In this course we use a simple 1.1%. Nordica doesn't keep it: FS-CD adds it to the bill, and it sits on 290100 until Nordica pays it to Skattestyrelsen, the Danish tax agency. Motor liability cover has its own separate tax, which we ignore here.

March 2026DKK
Written premium, all lines2,400,000.00
Insurance tax collected at 1.1% (to pay to Skattestyrelsen)26,400.00
Check with the Hygge Hjem policy: 1.1% × 120,0001,320.00

An insurer does not file an ordinary VAT return (momsangivelse) for its insurance business, because insurance is VAT-exempt. Instead the insurance tax is reported and paid, and the payroll tax (lønsumsafgift) has its own return. The deadlines and exact rules are for your tax team, so confirm with them. The finance job is to make sure the tax account 290100 matches the premium billed.

6.6 Steps 11 and 12: lock the period and review

Close the period (OB52): in Lesson 1 you saw that postings are only allowed into open posting periods. Peter now opens period 04.2026 for everyone and closes 03.2026, so nobody can post into March by accident. Often the period is first left open for a small finance group for a day or two, then closed for everyone.

Reports and review: Peter runs the P&L and balance sheet for March, the claims and premium reports by line of business, and the combined ratio from Lesson 4. He compares them with February and the budget, and asks "why?" for every big change. Then he signs off.

7. The financial statements of an insurer

All this work produces two main reports:

Profit and loss statement (P&L, income statement)

In simple words: a video of a period. For an insurer it starts with earned premium, takes off claims and running costs to give the technical result (the result of the insurance business itself), then adds investment income.

Balance sheet

In simple words: a photo on one day. What the company owns (assets) on one side, and who it belongs to on the other: outsiders (liabilities, including the reserves) and the owners (equity). Assets = liabilities + equity, always.

The two are linked: the result of the period becomes part of equity.

The result is shown before corporate income tax to keep it simple. The balance sheet is a deliberately simplified, made-up picture.

7.1 The March P&L by line of business

Nordica's lines of business (profit centres PC-MOTOR and PC-HOME) show the same layout. All figures are in DKK for March 2026:

Check: 1,600,000 − 1,060,000 − 350,000 = 190,000, and 190,000 + 40,000 = 230,000. Insurance tax is not in the P&L: it passes through to Skattestyrelsen.

The 350,000 operating expenses are made of the costs you met in earlier lessons: rent 50,000, telephony 18,000, IT 24,000, depreciation 5,000, the spring motor campaign 65,000, the laptops 100,000, and salaries and other costs 88,000 (including the 12,000 actuarial accrual). Check: 50 + 18 + 24 + 5 + 65 + 100 + 88 = 350 (thousand). The tiny DKK 100 currency gain is left out to keep the numbers round.

Combined ratio (Lesson 4): (claims incurred + operating expenses) ÷ earned premium = (1,060,000 + 350,000) ÷ 1,600,000 = 88.1%. Under 100% means the insurance business itself made money before investment income.

In SAP, the balance sheet can never be out of balance, because every document has debits equal to credits. If a report doesn't balance, look for a wrong report setup, not a wrong posting.

8. Year-end and reporting in brief

December is closed like any other month, plus some extra work. Fiscal year 2026 has 12 normal periods (March is period 3) and 4 special periods, 13 to 16, for year-end adjustments. The year-end close for 2026 happens in January and February 2027.

In S/4HANA, the old separate fiscal year change for assets is normally handled by the general balance carry-forward. Check your release.

An insurer also reports in ways other companies don't. IFRS 17 is the international accounting standard for insurance contracts. Solvency II and the Danish statutory reports to Finanstilsynet, the Danish Financial Supervisory Authority, check that Nordica has enough capital to pay its claims. These need specialised tools and teams: the detailed IFRS 17 measurement is usually done in a separate system that receives data from SAP. As a BA you don't need the detail. You do need to know that the SAP ledger is the source of the numbers, so the data must be right and traceable. Your actuarial and finance teams own the rest.

9. BA toolkit for assets, reserves and closing

9.1 Questions to ask

  • Which asset classes do we need, and which GL accounts does each one post to? From what amount is something an asset (the capitalisation limit)?
  • Which depreciation areas are needed (book, tax, group)? Who owns the tax rules?
  • How do UPR, RBNS and IBNR numbers reach SAP: a file, an interface or a manual journal? Who prepares, who approves, and what is the cut-off date?
  • Do the reserves have to be split by line of business (motor, home) and by reinsurance share? Are they posted gross and net?
  • When are the FS-CD transfers to the GL complete each month, and how is the 140000 balance reconciled to FS-CD?
  • What does the close checklist look like, with owners and deadlines for each task? Which tasks are automated?
  • Which accruals are posted every month, and how are they estimated?
  • Which foreign currencies and rate types are used for month-end valuation? Who owns the insurance tax and payroll tax reporting?
  • How many working days does finance have to close a month? Who may post after the period is closed?

A closing tip

Ask Peter for the close checklist in your first week, and sit with him for one month-end. Most "the numbers look wrong" questions you'll get as a BA can be answered by asking: which step of the close produced this posting, and was it run in the right order? For reserves, add one more: who owns this number, and where did the journal come from?
