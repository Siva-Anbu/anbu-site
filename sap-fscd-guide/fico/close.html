Fixed assets and month-end close

How Nordlys Møbler looks after its new CNC machine, spreads its cost over ten years, and closes the books for March 2026. Then a short look at what changes at year-end.

1. What a fixed asset is

A fixed asset is something a company buys to use, not to sell, and that it will use for more than one year. Machines, vans, buildings, office furniture and laptops are typical fixed assets. Oak timber is not: it goes into tables and leaves the company within weeks.

Why not just call the machine a DKK 600,000 expense in January? Because then January would look like a terrible month and the next nine years would look too good. The machine helps make tables for ten years, so its cost should be spread over those ten years. That spreading is called depreciation.

Depreciation

In simple words: each period, a slice of the asset's cost moves from the balance sheet (what the company owns) to the P&L (what it cost to run the business).

Example: DKK 600,000 ÷ 10 years = DKK 60,000 a year, or DKK 5,000 a month. This simple "same amount every period" method is called straight-line depreciation.

Book value (net book value)

In simple words: what the asset is still worth in the books: cost minus all depreciation so far.

Example: after one year, 600,000 − 60,000 = DKK 540,000. After ten years the book value is zero, even if the machine still works.

Straight-line depreciation of the CNC machine: the same DKK 60,000 slice every year until the book value reaches zero at the end of 2035.

2.1 Depreciation areas: one machine, two sets of rules

The financial statements and the Danish tax return don't depreciate the machine in the same way. Tax rules differ, so SAP keeps a separate depreciation area for each purpose. Each area has its own useful life, method and values for the same asset.

2.2 The asset sub-ledger

Remember suppliers and customers from Lessons 2 and 3? Each has its own detail (a sub-ledger), and the general ledger only sees the total on a reconciliation account. Assets work the same way. The asset sub-ledger knows every machine one by one. The general ledger only sees the totals on 211000 Machinery and equipment and 211900 Accumulated depreciation: machinery. Nobody posts to 211000 directly; every posting goes through an asset, so the two always agree.

The asset class "Machinery and equipment" points to 211000, 211900 and 481000 (this setup is called account determination). In S/4HANA all of it lands in the Universal Journal (ACDOCA).

3. Buying the machine (01.01.2026)

Nordlys bought the CNC machine from Maskin Nord A/S (a made-up machine supplier, BP 300180). The supplier invoice was DKK 600,000 plus 25% VAT. Because the invoice line points to asset 10000045 instead of a normal GL account, SAP updates the asset and the general ledger in one go. This is called an integrated acquisition: the asset, the supplier and the GL are all posted in one document.

4. The monthly depreciation run (AFAB)

Depreciation is not typed in by hand. Every month a batch program, the depreciation run (AFAB), calculates the planned amount for every asset and posts it to the general ledger. Peter runs it first as a test run, checks the list, then runs it for real. For the CNC machine it posts:

AccountDebit / CreditDKK
481000 Depreciation (cost centre 1000)Debit5,000.00
211900 Accumulated depreciation: machineryCredit5,000.00

Notice that 211000 is not touched: the original cost stays visible, and the depreciation piles up on its own account. Book value = 211000 minus 211900.

4.2 Selling or scrapping an asset

When an asset leaves the company, it is retired. SAP removes its cost and accumulated depreciation, and compares the book value with what the company got for it. Imagine Nordlys sells the CNC machine on 31.12.2028, after three years:

StepDKK
Cost600,000.00
Accumulated depreciation (36 months × 5,000)−180,000.00
Book value at sale420,000.00
Sold for (net, without VAT)450,000.00
Gain on sale (450,000 − 420,000)30,000.00

If it were sold for DKK 400,000 instead, there would be a loss of DKK 20,000. If it is simply scrapped for nothing, the whole book value is a loss. The sales invoice to the buyer also carries 25% output VAT, like any sale.

4.3 Assets under construction

Some assets are built over months, for example a new painting line. While it's being built, costs are collected on an asset under construction (AuC). An AuC is not depreciated, because it isn't in use yet. When the line is ready, the AuC is settled to a normal asset, and depreciation starts from then.

5. The month-end close checklist

Closing a month means: make sure everything that belongs in March is in March, check it, then lock March so nobody can change it. Peter's checklist has ten steps. The order matters, because later steps use the results of earlier ones.

Peter's close for March 2026. Real companies often have 40 or more tasks and track them in a closing tool (SAP has the Financial Closing cockpit, called Advanced Financial Closing in newer releases).

5.1 Steps 1 to 3: is everything in?

  1. 1. Sub-ledgers done (31.03): all March supplier invoices are posted (Maria checks her in-tray), and all March deliveries are billed. Hygge Hjem's billing of 15.03 is in, and its payment of 27.03 is cleared.
  2. 2. Bank reconciled (01.04): the last March bank statement from Danske Bank is loaded, and the balance on 110000 matches the bank's closing balance to the krone. Nothing is left on 113100 Bank incoming clearing.
  3. 3. GR/IR reviewed (01.04): Sofie lists open items on 191100 GR/IR clearing. The oak (goods received 05.03, invoiced 08.03) has cleared. Anything left over is either goods received but not yet invoiced, or invoiced but not yet received. Each one gets an owner and an explanation.

5.2 Step 4: accruals

The electricity for March has been used, but the supplier's invoice won't come until mid-April. If Nordlys waits for the invoice, March looks DKK 18,000 too cheap and April too expensive. (This is the DKK 18,000 you saw on cost centre 1000 in Lesson 4: it reached March through this accrual.) So Peter posts an accrual: a cost for March, owed to someone, without an invoice yet. It goes to a new account, 190000 Accrued expenses (a liability: money owed but not yet invoiced).

31.03.2026Debit / CreditDKK
473000 Electricity (cost centre 1000)Debit18,000.00
190000 Accrued expensesCredit18,000.00

The entry is posted with a reversal date of 01.04.2026. On 01.04 SAP posts the exact opposite. When the real invoice arrives in April and is posted to 473000, the reversal and the invoice cancel out, so April carries no electricity cost for March. March shows the cost once; April doesn't show it twice.

5.3 Steps 5 and 6: depreciation and foreign currency

Depreciation: Peter runs AFAB for period 03.2026 as described in section 4.

Foreign currency valuation (FAGL_FCV): on 20.03 Nordlys posted an invoice from a German supplier of table fittings for EUR 10,000. On that day the rate was 7.46, so the payable was booked at DKK 74,600. On 31.03 the rate is 7.45. The balance sheet must show what Nordlys would owe at today's rate:

CalculationDKK
Booked: 10,000 × 7.4674,600.00
Valued at month-end: 10,000 × 7.4574,500.00
Difference: Nordlys owes less, an unrealised gain100.00

FAGL_FCV posts the DKK 100: the payable goes down (debit, on a valuation adjustment account) and an unrealised exchange rate gain is credited to the P&L. "Unrealised" means it isn't real money yet: the rate can move again before Nordlys pays. Many companies reverse the valuation on the first day of the next month and value again at the next month-end. The accounts used are set up in configuration.

5.4 Steps 7 and 8: CO allocations and the VAT report

CO allocations: now that all March costs are in (including the accrual and depreciation), Jonas runs the IT assessment and the other cycles from Lesson 4, and settles internal order 700100 if needed. Running them earlier would have missed costs.

VAT report: the Danish VAT return (momsangivelse) is filed with SKAT, the Danish tax agency. It is output VAT minus input VAT for the period. In this story Nordlys reports monthly (large companies do; smaller ones report quarterly or half-yearly). Using only the two story invoices in March:

March 2026DKK
Output VAT on 175000 (sale to Hygge Hjem)30,000.00
Input VAT on 154000 (oak from Jysk Træ)−20,000.00
VAT to pay to SKAT10,000.00

In January it was the other way round: the machine's DKK 150,000 input VAT was much bigger than the output VAT, so Nordlys got money back. The VAT report in SAP lists every invoice by tax code, which is why correct tax codes on every invoice matter so much.

5.5 Steps 9 and 10: lock the period and review

Close the period (OB52): in Lesson 1 you saw that postings are only allowed into open posting periods. Peter now opens period 04.2026 for everyone and closes 03.2026, so nobody can post into March by accident. Often the period is first left open for a small finance group for a day or two, then closed for everyone.

Financial statements and review: Peter runs the balance sheet and P&L for March, compares them with February and with the budget, and asks "why?" for every big change. Then he signs off.

6. The financial statements in simple words

All this work produces two main reports:

Profit and loss statement (P&L, income statement)

In simple words: a video of a period. Revenue minus costs = profit (or loss) for the month or year.

Balance sheet

In simple words: a photo on one day. What the company owns (assets) on one side, and who it belongs to on the other: outsiders (liabilities, money owed) and the owners (equity). Assets = liabilities + equity, always.

The two are linked: the profit of the period becomes part of equity. That's why one wrong posting can make both reports wrong.

A deliberately tiny, made-up picture. A real P&L also has wages, materials, IT costs and much more.

Here is a tiny, made-up balance sheet for Nordlys on 31.03.2026. The bank and equity figures are invented to keep it small; the rest come from the story.

Add it up: assets 585,000 + 80,000 + 335,000 = 1,000,000. Liabilities 100,000 + 74,500 + 18,000 + 10,000 = 202,500. Equity 750,400 + 47,100 = 797,500. And 202,500 + 797,500 = 1,000,000. It balances. In SAP, the balance sheet can never be out of balance, because every document has debits equal to credits. If a report doesn't balance, look for a wrong report setup, not a wrong posting.

7. Year-end in brief

December is closed like any other month, plus some extra work. The year-end close for 2026 will happen in January and February 2027.

Special periods use the posting date 31.12 but are kept apart from December, so December's own figures stay clean.

8. BA toolkit for assets and closing

8.1 Questions to ask

  • Which asset classes do we need, and which GL accounts does each one post to?
  • Which depreciation areas are needed (book, tax, group)? Who owns the tax rules?
  • From what amount is something an asset rather than an expense (a capitalisation limit, for example DKK 30,000)?
  • Are assets bought with purchase orders? Who creates the asset master record, and when?
  • What does the close checklist look like, with owners and deadlines for each task? Which tasks are automated?
  • Which accruals are posted every month, and how are they estimated?
  • Which foreign currencies and rate types are used for month-end valuation? Where do the rates come from?
  • How many working days does finance have to close a month? Who may post after the period is closed?

Ask Peter for the close checklist on your first week, and sit with him for one month-end. Most "the numbers look wrong" questions you'll get as a BA can be answered by asking: which step of the close produced this posting, and was it run in the right order?
