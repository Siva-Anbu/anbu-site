Nordic Payment Methods in SAP FSCD

How insurance customers in Denmark, Sweden and Norway pay, how insurers pay them back, and what SAP FSCD does at each step. Simple words, simple pictures.

Before you start

Read the main FSCD guide first if words like open item, clearing or payment run are new. This page builds on them. All names, amounts and numbers are made up for learning.

Meet the three customers

🇩🇰 Mette Jensen, AarhusCar insurance at ABC Forsikring. DKK 8,088 a year, paid by Betalingsservice.
🇸🇪 Erik Andersson, UppsalaHome insurance (hemförsäkring). SEK 300 a month, paid by Autogiro.
🇳🇴 Ingrid Hansen, BergenCar insurance. NOK 900 a month, paid by AvtaleGiro.

Three countries, three currencies (DKK, SEK, NOK; none of them use the euro), but the same idea everywhere: FSCD tells the bank what to collect or pay, and the bank tells FSCD what actually happened.

1. The big picture: how money moves

FSCD never touches real money. It only sends instructions (files) and reads results (files). The banks and the national payment systems move the money.

Green = premiums coming in. Orange = claims and refunds going out. FSCD is the record-keeper at the end of the chain.

Every payment method on this page is just a different way to travel along this chain. They differ in three simple things:

QuestionWhy a BA cares
Who starts the payment, insurer or customer?If the insurer starts it (direct debit), FSCD's payment run creates a file. If the customer starts it (app, transfer), FSCD must recognise incoming money.
How is the payment linked to the right customer?Through a reference number (KID, OCR, FI payment ID) or a mandate. Bad reference = manual clarification work.
How fast does it arrive, and how does FSCD hear about it?Instant (Swish), same day, or on a fixed date. FSCD learns through a result file or the bank statement.

3. Direct debit: the insurer collects

This is the most common way to pay insurance in all three countries. The customer gives permission once (a mandate, called tilmelding, medgivande or avtale). After that, FSCD's payment run collects every premium on the due date.

Steps 1 to 5 are the happy path. Step 6 is the "bounce" from the main guide: a return re-opens the item.

🇩🇰 Betalingsservice (Denmark)

In simple words: Denmark's national direct debit, run by Nets (part of Mastercard). Nearly every household uses it for rent, electricity and insurance. The customer sees all upcoming payments in a monthly overview from their bank.

Example: Mette signs up once in her netbank using her customer number from ABC. On 15.04 FSCD's payment run includes her DKK 8,088. Nets collects it and tells ABC the result. If her account is empty, a return comes back and FSCD adds the DKK 100 fee.

🇸🇪 Autogiro (Sweden)

In simple words: Sweden's direct debit, run through Bankgirot. The customer gives a medgivande (consent), often digitally with BankID. Autogiro is ideal for monthly premiums.

Example: Erik pays SEK 300 on the 28th of every month. FSCD's monthly run sends the collection to Bankgirot. On the 28th the money moves; the next day Bankgirot's report tells FSCD "paid" or "not paid", and FSCD clears or re-opens the item.

🇳🇴 AvtaleGiro (Norway)

In simple words: Norway's direct debit, run by the banks with Nets. The customer sets a maximum amount they allow per payment, and usually gets a notice before each debit. It's often combined with eFaktura.

Example: Ingrid allows up to NOK 2,000 per payment. Her NOK 900 premium is collected on the 20th. If ABC ever tried to collect NOK 2,500 (say, after a price change), the bank would refuse it. So a BA must check the maximum when premiums go up.

What FSCD stores for direct debit

The customer's incoming payment method on the contract account (for example "Betalingsservice"), the mandate details and the bank account on the Business Partner. The payment run (FPY1) reads these to decide who goes into which collection file.

4. Bills with a reference number: the customer pays

Here the customer starts the payment: they get a bill and pay it in their netbank. The magic ingredient is the reference number printed on the bill. It tells FSCD exactly whose money it is.

A good reference number turns a payment into an automatic match. A missing one costs manual work: this is the biggest everyday pain in collections.

All three numbers end with a check digit, so the bank can reject a mistyped number before the money is even sent. In FSCD the number is usually built from the contract account or document number, so FSCD can turn it straight back into "this is Ingrid's premium".

Example: Erik pays a bill in Sweden

Erik's Autogiro failed one month, so ABC sends him a bill for SEK 300 with Bankgiro number and OCR number. He pays in his netbank. That night Bankgirot sends ABC a file of all incoming payments (the BgMax file). FSCD loads it as a payment lot, reads the OCR number, finds Erik's open item and clears it. If Erik had typed a free-text message instead of the OCR number, his payment would sit in clarification.

5. E-invoices: the bill lives in the netbank

An e-invoice is the same bill, but delivered straight into the customer's netbank (or digital mailbox) instead of on paper. The customer just presses "approve". The reference number travels along automatically, so matching is easy.

  • 🇸🇪 E-faktura: very popular in Sweden. Customers also get bills in the digital mailbox Kivra.
  • 🇳🇴 eFaktura: very popular in Norway; often combined with AvtaleGiro so the bill is shown and paid automatically. Digital mailbox: Digipost.
  • 🇩🇰 Denmark: Betalingsservice already shows the bill, and letters go to e-Boks.

For a BA: FSCD (or the print/output system next to it) must produce the invoice data in the right format and send it to the e-invoice provider. Dunning letters often use the same digital channels.

6. Mobile payments: MobilePay, Swish, Vipps

Phone apps linked to the customer's bank account. They are fast and loved by customers, especially for one-off payments: a missed premium, a deductible, a small new policy.

The money arrives fast, but FSCD still learns about it through a file, so the policy number in the payment is what makes it match.

AppCountrySimple insurance example
MobilePay🇩🇰 Denmark (also Finland)Mette's direct debit bounced; she pays the DKK 8,188 with MobilePay from a link in the reminder SMS.
Swish🇸🇪 SwedenErik buys travel insurance for SEK 450 on ABC's website and pays with Swish in seconds.
Vipps🇳🇴 NorwayIngrid pays her NOK 3,000 deductible (egenandel) after a claim, using Vipps.
Good to know

Vipps (Norway) and MobilePay (Denmark, Finland) merged into one company, Vipps MobilePay, but the two apps still exist under their own names. Swish is owned by the big Swedish banks. App payments can also be recurring (subscriptions) in some setups, working like a direct debit.

7. Money out: paying claims and refunds

When ABC pays a customer, the direction flips. A claims system (or a cancellation) creates a payable in FSCD, and the outgoing payment run sends a payment file to ABC's bank.

Same chain as section 1, travelled in the orange direction.

CountryUsual payout methodExample
🇩🇰 DenmarkBank transfer to the customer's account (registration number + account number), often instantMette's DKK 25,000 car claim is paid to her account at her bank.
🇸🇪 SwedenBank transfer, or Swish payout to the customer's mobile numberErik's stolen bike: SEK 3,500 paid by Swish payout. It arrives in seconds.
🇳🇴 NorwayBank transfer to the customer's 11-digit account numberIngrid's windscreen claim: NOK 6,000 paid straight to the glass repair shop.

Many banks now accept payment files in the international ISO 20022 format (the "pain.001" file for outgoing payments). In SAP these files are created by the Payment Medium Workbench, so one FSCD payment run can produce files for all three countries.

8.1 One day in FSCD, across three countries

  1. Night before: payment run (FPY1) creates a Betalingsservice file (DKK), an Autogiro file (SEK) and an AvtaleGiro file (NOK), plus one payout file for approved claims.
  2. Morning: files from Nets, Bankgirot and the banks arrive. FSCD loads incoming payments as payment lots (FP05) and failed debits as return lots (FP09).
  3. Automatic matching: payments with good KID, OCR or FI numbers clear their open items. Returns re-open items and add fees.
  4. During the day: the operations team works the clarification queue: payments without a usable reference.
  5. Evening: totals per currency go to the general ledger, and dunning picks up anything still unpaid.

9. BA checklist for a Nordic payment requirement

  1. Which country and currency? Each has its own schemes, file formats and cut-off times.
  2. Which payment family: direct debit, reference bill, e-invoice, mobile or card?
  3. How is the reference number built, and does it include a check digit?
  4. Where is the mandate stored, and what happens when a customer cancels it in their netbank?
  5. What are the dates: when must the collection file reach the scheme so the money moves on the due date?
  6. What happens on failure: return reasons, fees, retry, dunning, digital-mailbox reminders?
  7. For Norway: what if the premium is higher than the customer's AvtaleGiro maximum?
  8. For payouts: is the account number or mobile number validated before the run?
  9. How will we test it? Ask the bank for a test environment and sample files.
