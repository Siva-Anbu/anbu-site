Dunning, returns and collections

What happens when customers don't pay: reminders, bounced payments, payment plans, write-offs and the Danish rules behind them. Told with the same customer, Mette.

Before you start

This lesson builds on Lesson 1: FSCD basics. You should know what an open item, a due date and a return are. All names, amounts and codes are made up for learning.

Where we left Mette

Mette Jensen in Aarhus owes ABC Forsikring DKK 8,088 for her car insurance, due on 15.04.2026. Her Betalingsservice debit bounced on 18.04 and FSCD added a DKK 100 return fee, so she now owes DKK 8,188. In Lesson 1 she paid by MobilePay on 06.05. In this lesson we slow down and look at everything FSCD does while she hasn't paid, and what happens if she never does.

1. Why collections matter

Most customers pay on time. A small share doesn't, and that share costs real money: lost premium, cover that must be stopped, angry customers and manual work. Collections in FSCD has three goals:

GoalIn simple wordsMette example
Get paidRemind the customer, politely and then firmlyAn email and SMS on day 14 gets most people to pay
Protect the companyStop cover when premium is not paid, so ABC doesn't insure for freeAfter the final notice, her motor cover ends
Treat customers fairlyFollow the law, give time, offer payment plans, don't chase disputed amountsIf she disputes the fee, dunning stops until it's sorted

2. The dunning procedure: levels, days and charges

A dunning procedure is the rulebook for reminders. It says how many levels there are, how many days after the due date each level starts, what is sent, and whether a fee is charged. Each contract account points to one procedure, so motor and home insurance can have different rules.

An example ladder. Your project's days, texts and fees will differ; the shape is usually the same.

3. How a dunning run works: proposal, then activity

Dunning isn't done by hand. A nightly batch job does it in two steps, so people can check the list before anything is sent.

Many projects run both steps automatically every night and only look at the proposal when something looks wrong.

  1. 29.04 (day 14): the proposal finds Mette's DKK 8,188 open and overdue. The activity run sends level 1 by email and SMS. No fee. A dunning history record is saved; no posting document, because no money moved.
  2. 06.05: Mette pays by MobilePay. Her items are cleared, so the next run doesn't pick her up. Dunning stops by itself.
  3. If she hadn't paid, 15.05 (day 30): level 2 letter in e-Boks and a DKK 100 fee. The fee is posted as a new open item, so the account now shows DKK 8,288.

4. Rules that stop or change dunning

Not every overdue item should be chased. FSCD has several "brakes". Knowing them saves hours of incident analysis ("why didn't this customer get a reminder?").

Dunning lock

In simple words: a "do not remind" flag, on one item or the whole account, often with an end date and a reason.

Example: Mette calls and says the DKK 100 fee is wrong because her bank made a mistake. Customer service sets a dunning lock on the fee item with reason "disputed" until 31.05, so she gets no reminder about it while it's checked.

Grace days and minimum amounts

In simple words: small delays and tiny amounts are ignored, so customers aren't nagged about DKK 3.

Example: with a DKK 50 minimum, a leftover DKK 12 after a rounding difference is never dunned. It is written off instead (section 7).

Open claims or credits

In simple words: if ABC owes the customer money, it's unfair to chase them. Many projects offset first or pause dunning.

Example: Mette has an approved DKK 25,000 claim waiting. Instead of dunning, FSCD offsets: she receives DKK 16,812 and her premium is cleared.

Instalment plan

In simple words: items inside an active payment plan follow the plan's dates instead of the original due date (section 6).

BA tip: "why no reminder?" checklist

Check in this order: is the item really overdue (due date and grace days)? Is the amount above the minimum? Is there a dunning lock on the item or account? Is it in an instalment plan? Did the last run actually include this account (run selection)? Is a credit or claim being offset?

5. Returns: when a payment bounces

A return is a payment the bank sends back. Every return comes with a return reason, and FSCD can react differently to each one. This is set up in a table that a BA usually helps define.

Return reasons and their actions are configuration. A BA writes down the business rule for each reason.

6. Instalment plans: helping customers pay

An instalment plan splits one or more overdue items into smaller amounts with new due dates. The original items are "parked" inside the plan, and the plan's instalments become the new open items. Dunning then follows the plan. If the customer misses an instalment, the plan can be cancelled and the full amount is due again.

Example: Mette asks for help

On 10.05 Mette calls: she lost her job and can't pay DKK 8,188 at once. The collections agent creates an instalment plan (FPR1): 3 instalments on 01.06, 01.07 and 01.08.

BA questions for instalment plans: who may create them (call centre, self-service)? Maximum number of instalments? Is interest or a fee charged? Can disputed items be included? What happens to cover while a plan is running? What happens after one missed instalment?

7. The end of the road: cancellation, collection agency, write-off

If nothing works, three things can happen. They often happen in this order.

StepIn simple wordsMette example (if she never paid)
Cancellation for non-paymentFSCD tells the policy system that premium is still unpaid after the final notice. The policy system stops the cover and works out what is really owed for the period that was insured.Cover ends on 21.06. She owes premium for April to June only; the rest of the year is credited back.
Hand over to collection (inkasso)The remaining debt is sent to an internal recovery team or an outside debt-collection agency. FSCD marks the items as "submitted to collection" and later records what the agency recovered.DKK 2,200 is submitted to the agency on 15.07; DKK 1,500 is recovered in September.
Write-offMoney that will never be collected is written off. The open item is closed against a "bad debt" expense account, so the books show the loss honestly.The last DKK 700 is written off in December (FP04).
Small differences are written off automatically

Many projects run an automatic write-off for tiny leftovers, for example anything under DKK 10 that is more than 60 days old. It saves letters that cost more than the debt.

8. Danish rules a BA must know

Collections is regulated. Rules change, so always confirm the current text with your compliance team. These are the usual points to check for Denmark:

  • Reminder fees are capped by law. The Danish Interest Act (renteloven) limits reminder fees per letter (DKK 100 at the time of writing), the number of fee-bearing reminders, and requires a gap of at least 10 days between them. FSCD's dunning charges must stay within these limits.
  • Cover can only stop after a proper written reminder. Under the Insurance Contracts Act (forsikringsaftaleloven), the reminder must clearly say that cover will stop if the premium isn't paid by a stated deadline. So the wording of the final notice is a legal requirement, not just a template.
  • Digital post counts. Letters sent to e-Boks are normally treated like paper letters, which is why most Danish insurers use it for levels 2 and 3.
  • Debt collection has its own rules. Agencies need permission, and extra collection costs are limited. FSCD only records what was handed over and what came back.
